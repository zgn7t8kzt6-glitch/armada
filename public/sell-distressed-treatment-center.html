Insights · The Fire Sale

Selling a distressed treatment center? Read this first.

Windward Operating Partners · For owners weighing an exit under pressure

Nobody plans to sell their facility at the bottom. But that's when the calls come — when census has slipped, the advances are debiting daily, and a broker or a roll-up's corp-dev person says the magic words: "We can move fast."

Before you sign anything, you should understand exactly what "fast" costs, because the math of selling in distress is the most expensive math in this industry.

What distress does to your price

Treatment facilities are valued as a multiple of EBITDA — and both numbers collapse at once in distress:

  • The EBITDA is your worst year. Buyers price off trailing twelve months. If census slipped and collections wobbled, you're selling the weakest financial snapshot your facility has ever produced.
  • The multiple is punished too. Healthy platforms with a full continuum command roughly 9–12x EBITDA. Struggling programs trade near 3–4x a depressed number — when they get a real multiple at all. Some distressed sales are effectively asset deals: license, building, beds, goodbye.
  • Your debt comes off the top. Stacked advances and liens get paid out of proceeds at closing. Owners sometimes walk away from a sale with almost nothing after years of building.
The spread is the whole story: the gap between a distressed multiple on a depressed EBITDA and a healthy multiple on a recovered EBITDA is the single largest sum of money that will ever move in your facility's life — and a fire sale hands all of it to the buyer.

What buyers in distress won't volunteer

  • They are pricing your desperation, not your facility. The faster you need to close, the lower the number goes.
  • The recovery is their plan, not a mystery. The buyer intends to fix census, tighten collections, and sell or hold at a much higher value. They just don't intend to share it with you.
  • Retrades are common. A distressed seller who's already mentally exited has no leverage when the price drops 20% in diligence.
  • Earn-outs favor the buyer. If part of your price depends on performance after you've given up control, read it twice and assume the worst case.

The questions that protect you

  • What would this facility be worth at a stabilized census — and who captures that difference in this deal?
  • What happens to my debt at closing, line by line?
  • Is there a structure where I keep a stake in the recovery instead of selling all of it at the bottom?

That last question is the one most owners never hear. A full sale at distressed pricing is not the only structure. In a recapitalization, a partner puts capital into the facility — retiring the advances, securing payroll — takes operating control to run the turnaround, and the owner keeps meaningful equity. You give up part of the facility at today's honest price; you keep a share of what it becomes. We've written up what a real turnaround actually takes, and if debt is the main driver, start with the MCA spiral and the way out.

When selling outright IS the right call

Honesty cuts both ways: if you're done — no energy left for a partnership, no desire to stay in the industry — a clean exit with a specialist broker beats a partnership you don't want. And if the facility genuinely can't be turned (wrong market, wrong building, wrong licenses), no structure fixes that. The point isn't that selling is wrong. It's that selling at the bottom, without pricing the recovery, should be a choice you make with the alternatives in front of you — not the only door someone showed you.
