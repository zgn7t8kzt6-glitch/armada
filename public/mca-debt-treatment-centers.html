Insights · The MCA Trap

Merchant cash advance debt is killing treatment centers. Here's the way out.

Windward Operating Partners · For owners of behavioral health & addiction treatment facilities

It starts the same way almost every time. Census dips for a quarter. A payer slows down payments. Payroll Friday gets uncomfortably close, and an email arrives — "$250K in your account in 48 hours, no collateral, no credit check." For an owner staring at a payroll shortfall, it reads like a lifeline.

It isn't. It's the first turn of a spiral that has put treatment centers across the country out of business — and if you're reading this with one or more advances already debiting your account, you already know how it feels from the inside.

How the merchant cash advance spiral works

A merchant cash advance (MCA) isn't technically a loan — it's a purchase of your future revenue at a discount, which is how funders route around usury laws. The mechanics that matter:

  • Factor rates of 1.2–1.5 mean you repay $120K–$150K for every $100K received. Because repayment happens over months, not years, the effective APR typically lands between 40% and over 300%.
  • Daily debits pull money from your operating account every business day, destroying the float between when revenue arrives and when bills are due.
  • Blanket UCC liens get filed against your business — receivables, equipment, everything — making ordinary bank financing nearly impossible afterward.
  • Confessions of judgment in some agreements let the funder take a judgment against you without a trial if you miss payments.

The spiral is structural: the first advance's daily debits create the exact cash shortage that makes the second advance feel necessary. Struggling businesses routinely end up with three to seven stacked advances consuming 30–60% of gross revenue. At that point the facility is working for the funders — not the patients, not the staff, not the owner.

Why it's worse in treatment: behavioral health revenue arrives on insurance timelines — 30, 60, 90 days after care is delivered — but MCA debits come out daily. The mismatch is brutal for exactly the businesses MCA brokers target hardest.

Why the usual escapes rarely work

Refinancing is hard because the blanket liens scare off banks, and "reverse consolidation" offers are usually just another advance wearing a disguise. Settlement firms can negotiate balances down, but many charge heavy fees and the facility's underlying problem — operations that can't support its cost structure — remains untouched. Riding it out means cutting staff to make the debits, which pushes census down further, which is how facilities die slowly rather than quickly.

The uncomfortable truth: an MCA stack is almost never the disease. It's the symptom of a facility whose census, collections, or cost structure broke first. Any real fix has to address both the debt and the operations — at the same time.

What the real options look like

If your facility carries stacked advances, you have roughly four doors:

  • Another advance. Buys weeks, deepens the hole.
  • Sell the facility. Possible — but distressed facilities trade at the bottom of the market, and the advances get paid off out of your proceeds. See our companion piece: Selling a distressed treatment center? Read this first.
  • Restructure and grind. With a good healthcare restructuring attorney, balances can sometimes be renegotiated — if the underlying operations can recover fast enough to matter.
  • Recapitalize with a capital-and-operations partner. New equity capital retires the advances at closing — liens released, daily debits stopped — while an embedded operating team fixes what broke the cash flow in the first place. The owner keeps a stake and shares the recovery. This is the model Windward exists to provide.

The questions to ask before you take anyone's money

  • What is the effective APR, not the factor rate? (Make them compute it.)
  • What gets filed against my business, and when is it released?
  • Does this money fix the problem that created the shortfall — or just move the cliff?
  • Is the person offering it paid when I sign, or paid when I recover?

That last one is the entire game. An advance funder is paid from your collections whether you recover or not. A partner who only profits if the facility becomes worth more is on your side of the table by construction.
