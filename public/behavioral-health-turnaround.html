What a behavioral health turnaround actually takes. (It's not marketing.)

Windward Operating Partners · Written from inside facilities we own and operate

When census slips, the first instinct in this industry is always the same: spend more on marketing. More ads, a new agency, another directory listing. And it almost never works — because struggling facilities almost never have a marketing problem. They have an operations problem wearing a marketing costume.

This is what we've learned turning facilities around inside our own multi-site platform — hundreds of beds, detox through outpatient. The census follows the operations. Six failures account for most of the gap between the facility you have and the facility you built.

1. Your best people are interviewing elsewhere

Frontline turnover is the most expensive line item nobody budgets for. Every departing clinician takes referral relationships, institutional knowledge, and patient trust out the door — and tells the market something about your facility. Census follows culture, not the other way around. The fix is recognition, ownership, and career structure, applied daily — not a pizza party after a bad quarter.

2. Patients leave before treatment works

High AMA rates are treated as clinical weather. They're not — they're an operations problem with operations causes. In one of our own detox units, weekend AMA rates ran three times weekday rates, a pattern invisible in the monthly average until we split the data. Root causes: thinner weekend engagement, unclear after-hours authority, nobody empowered to solve a problem at 2 a.m. Saturday. An authority charter for charge nurses, a paid on-call structure, and weekend programming built to Tuesday's standard brought it down. Averages lie. Split the data until the problem shows itself.

3. Beds sit empty while calls go unanswered

The fastest census gains come from demand you already have: alumni, readmits, families who called once and never heard back. Walk your own intake process as a caller sometime. Most struggling facilities are leaking admissions through a process nobody owns end to end — while paying for ads to generate more calls to leak.

4. One referral source owns your census

If a third of your admissions come from one relationship, you don't have a pipeline — you have a hostage situation, and the ransom gets paid in discounts, favors, and fear. Diversification is slow, unglamorous relationship work. It's also the only thing that turns a referral crisis from fatal to survivable.

5. Leadership finds out about problems in month-end reports

In the best-run hospitals, problems surface in hours — a daily management system makes the numbers known every single day, with explicit ownership for every metric. In struggling facilities, problems surface in month-end reports, thirty days after they started compounding. By the time a bad month is visible, it's a bad quarter.

6. Doing the work, not getting paid for it

Documentation gaps, authorization breakdowns, and billing-process failures quietly bleed facilities dry. In one facility of ours, a single broken discharge process was costing $130K a month — census looked healthy while revenue collapsed, because discharges were being suppressed to protect the census number and patients aged out of higher levels of care. The fix wasn't marketing: discharge integrity, level-of-care discipline, and changing what leadership measured.

Why most turnarounds fail anyway

Because advice isn't operations. A consultant's binder doesn't show up on the Saturday night shift. Turnarounds fail when nobody has the authority to change things daily, when the fix is a report instead of a system, and when the money runs out before the operations recover — which is why facilities drowning in advance debt can't fix operations with cuts, and why selling at the bottom hands the recovery to someone else. Capital and operations have to arrive together, with authority, and stay until the system holds.
